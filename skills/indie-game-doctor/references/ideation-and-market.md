# Ideation and Market Fit

Reference for the concept stage: picking a candidate, killing one, and telling a team why
an idea will not sell before they spend a year finding out. Not prototyping method, not
scope discipline, not marketing tactics — see the other reference files for those.

---

## 1. Evaluate a candidate concept

Run every candidate through this in order. Stop at the first failure.

1. **Say it as a fantasy, not a mechanic.** "Run a drug empire" survives. "Water plants"
   does not, even though watering plants is the actual verb (*Schedule I*). A verb list
   with no fantasy wrapped around it fails here.
2. **Readability test.** Does a screenshot or a two-second clip communicate genre,
   mechanic, and fantasy at once? *A Difficult Game About Climbing* and *Snakebird* pass
   cold. If it needs a caption, it fails.
3. **Name the primary Magic** (section 3). A concept with none of the six is not a
   concept yet, it's a mechanic.
4. **Name the genre and its demand tier** (section 4). A hard genre isn't disqualifying;
   it changes what has to be budgeted before the concept is viable.
5. **If it combines genres, name the method** (section 5) and state which weakness
   cancels which. "Genre A meets genre B" with no cancellation is a feature list.
6. **Check every addition against the fantasy** (section 6). An addition that clashes
   with the core appeal subtracts, even if it's well made.
7. **The Lucas Pope test.** Can the team already picture the trailer? If not, the concept
   isn't concrete enough to prototype yet.

A concept that passes all seven still has to clear scope and runway separately. This
checklist only answers "is this worth prototyping."

## 2. Appeal, Fun, and Scope predict different things

Teams routinely diagnose a weak concept as a weak prototype, or a weak prototype as a
marketing problem, because they don't separate what each term predicts.

| Term | Predicts | Doesn't predict | Fixed in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Appeal** | Whether a stranger wants it before touching it — wishlists, clicks, "I want to be/explore/tinker/break/master/hear that." | Whether the game is fun once played. | Concept, fantasy, art direction, market gap. |
| **Fun** | Whether a stranger keeps playing once started — flow, challenge matching skill. | Whether anyone finds the game at all. | Mechanics, feedback loops, pacing. |
| **Scope** | Whether the team can finish it, and whether Appeal and Fun survive production scale. | Quality directly — it's a cost term. | Production planning. |

Appeal and Fun are close to independent. A game can be 100% fun — full flow — with zero
fantasy wrapped around it; fantasy sells the game, it doesn't generate the flow. A game
can also have enormous appeal and turn out not fun once played. Diagnosing "no players" as
a Fun problem when it's actually Appeal (or the reverse) sends the team to fix the wrong
system.

Quick read: existing playtesters enjoying it but nobody new arriving is Appeal. People
wishlisting and bouncing in the first session is Fun. Either fix not fitting the runway is
Scope, regardless of which of the other two is broken.

Shorthand, as Tyroller states it: **(Fun × Appeal) / Scope**. Zero on either multiplied
term zeroes the product; Scope never zeroes it but can make the whole thing arrive too late
or too thin. This skill adds no marketing term to that formula, because the corpus has no
sourced weight for one. The 90/10 product-versus-promotion split people reach for here is
rhetoric with no methodology behind it; see `numbers.md` §6.

## 3. The six Magics

Every viable concept carries at least one. Name the primary; a second is a bonus. Polish
is not on this list — it converts and retains, it doesn't spark interest.

| # | Magic | What it is | Sells on | Demands |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Distinct graphics | A cohesive, novel aesthetic, not raw fidelity (*Blasphemous*, *Laysara: Summit Kingdom*). | Screenshots, socials, capsule art. | Visual consistency across every asset — the art has to be the pitch. |
| 2 | High-concept hook | A one-sentence pitch does the whole job (*Untitled Goose Game*, *Squirrel with a Gun*, *Choo-Choo Charles*). | The sentence itself, repeated. | Has to survive being said out loud after a ten-second glance. Needing a second clause means it isn't high-concept yet. |
| 3 | Physics comedy | Emergent, shareable chaos (*Totally Accurate Battle Simulator*, *Garry's Mod*). | Clips, GIFs, streamer reactions. | Systems must generate surprising moments on their own, repeatably. |
| 4 | Addictive loop | "Just one more run" (*Vampire Survivors*, *Dome Keeper*). | Hands-on play, not screenshots. | A demo is mandatory; median demo playtime is the metric, not wishlist count. |
| 5 | Deep systems | Tinkering depth for players who set their own goals (*Cosmoteer*, Dwarf Fortress). | Word of mouth in a tinkerer community; can succeed on minimal production (Dwarf Fortress shipped on ASCII). | The depth has to be real — a shallow system dressed as deep gets found out fast. |
| 6 | Starved niche | A beloved genre with no good recent entries (*Stardew Valley* against an absent *Harvest Moon* pipeline). | Existing fans searching for exactly this. | Genuine competence in the genre's fundamentals; the niche is starved for quality, not novelty. |

Magics 1–3 are screenshot-sellable: budget goes to capsule art, key art, a short trailer.
Magics 4–6 are demo-sellable: budget goes into the demo, because the sell happens during
play. A concept with none of the six isn't lacking marketing, it's lacking a reason to be
looked at twice — a concept-stage finding, not a launch-stage one.

## 4. Genre demand: structurally hard genres and traps

Genres carry unequal baseline demand independent of execution quality. This changes what
a concept has to pay for before it's viable, not whether the genre is allowed.

**Structurally hard** (oversaturated, weak baseline purchasing, or a small demanding
niche): 2D/3D platformers, puzzle games, VR, traditional RTS, multiplayer/party, shmups,
traditional 2D beat-'em-ups. These need top-1% visual craft to have a chance — the genre
gets compared to its own historical apex regardless of what a newcomer intended to compete
on.

**Winner-take-all** (judged against the genre's defining title, not "competent"):
roguelike deckbuilders, character-action, large Metroidvanias, battle royales.
Platformers and Metroidvanias specifically get compared to *Ori and the Blind Forest* and
*Hollow Knight* — "good" reliably lands at single-digit reviews without matching that tier
or a radically distinct hook.

**Candy-aisle** (high consumption, impulse-bought against tropes and tags, no
genre-defining execution required): horror, incremental/idle, visual novels/narrative,
impulse co-op/party. Easier to sell, not easier to make good.

**Demand-leading traps**: the content-dense high-demand genres, which the corpus names as
deckbuilders, roguelites and grand strategy, plus city builders and simulation. You cannot
enter one and quietly ship 20% of the expected content volume; the audience treats content
generosity as the competitive axis. The demand signal is real and the production math does
not close for a small team.

**Genre salad.** Combining more genres than the pitch can hold in one sentence (rhythm +
horror + dating sim + deckbuilder) alienates each component genre's fans with the others'
mechanics instead of pooling them. If the pitch needs a second sentence of setup, cut
until one clause survives against a genre buyers already recognize.

**Follow the blueprint.** For a first or second title, execute inside an established
genre's conventions. Put differentiation in fantasy, art cohesion, or an underserved niche
inside the genre, not in the rules; save mechanical invention for later titles. This sits
in tension with "find a gap in the market" — the reconciliation is that a market gap is a
positioning choice, not a mechanical invention (Islanders found a gap in hyper-minimalist
chill city-builders without inventing a mechanic to do it).

For execution-only versus differentiation-required strategy, and when each wins, see
`verdicts.md` §2.

## 5. Combining genres: three methods

A combination that stacks two feature lists is scope cost with no synergy. A combination
that lets one genre's strength cancel the other's known weakness is a real concept.

**1. Hand-off (sequential switching).** The game alternates between distinct genre
modules — *Persona*, *XCOM*, *Shovel Knight: King of Cards*. Cheapest of the three; the
modules never run simultaneously.
- Works when the secondary mode is short, optional, or skippable after repeated failure.
- Fails on the Covert Action rule: a secondary minigame that runs too long or too deep
  costs the game its throughline back to the loop it interrupts.
- Fails when players bought genre A and get forced into genre B with no warning.
- Best version interlocks the loops so one feeds the other, not merely alternates.

**2. Play style (systemic parallelism).** One world, multiple genre toolkits, player
picks an approach — *Deus Ex*, *Dishonored*, *Prey*. Most expensive: supporting three
playstyles is near-equivalent to building three games' worth of content, AI, and
animation, and under-resourcing shows.
- Fails on the jack-of-all-trades trap: judged against a dedicated single-genre title,
  the hybrid loses on every individual axis. Not a flaw to fix — the structural cost of
  the method, and it has to be budgeted for.
- Fails when a mandatory encounter can only be solved one way, breaking the freedom the
  concept sold.
- Works when every encounter is beatable through each supported playstyle and the game is
  marketed as a toolkit, not as isolated mechanics.

**3. Blend (true mechanical fusion).** Mechanics from two genres fuse into one indivisible
loop — *Spelunky*, *Crypt of the NecroDancer*, *Portal*. Highest payoff: genuine
low-competition positioning, hard to copy.
- The **Cancellation Principle** is what makes a blend work, not just exist: genre A's
  known weakness is exactly what genre B's strength cancels, and vice versa, without
  either compromising the other. Named for *Spelunky*: platformers suffer repetitive
  memorization, roguelike procgen fixes that; roguelikes suffer opaque UI and complex
  controls, platformer-style immediate input fixes that.
- Fails on philosophical incompatibility: procgen can dilute handcrafted spatial
  discovery (roguelike procgen mixed into a Metroidvania); gear-score systems can
  invalidate an instant-action power fantasy.
- Fastest way to find a candidate blend: look for two genres that already share
  foundational geometry or input logic — a grid, a turn structure, a pacing rhythm.

A concept that names its method and states which weakness cancels which beats a pitch
that just lists "genre A meets genre B."

## 6. Innovation that helps versus innovation that clashes

"Be different" is not a design principle on its own.

- **Functional innovation** recombines proven components so they complement the genre's
  core appeal. *Dungeon Clawler* keeps the deckbuilder-roguelike frame and replaces
  card-draw with a claw machine — familiar enough to grasp instantly, novel enough to
  stand out. This sits at the intersection of familiarity and novelty; too familiar reads
  as derivative, too novel reads as alienating.
- **Clashing innovation** adds a mechanic that fights the genre's core appeal even when
  well executed on its own. A progression and attack-customization system layered onto a
  Vampire-Survivors-style game undermined the genre's actual draw — immediate simplicity,
  fast dopamine — because the addition competed with what players came for.

The test for any proposed addition: does it serve the concept's core fantasy, or does it
just add a feature the genre "usually has"? A vision-statement razor makes this concrete —
one sentence naming the target feeling (Subnautica: "thrill of the unknown"; FTL:
"commanding a starship"; Flower: "relaxation, calm, peace"), then test every proposed
mechanic against it, including genre furniture like autosave, minimap, or a skill tree.
*Flower* stripped level-ups, spells, and timers because they fought its calm vision, not
because they were badly made.

- **Symptom:** a well-made addition makes the game feel worse once it's in. **Diagnosis:**
  clashing innovation. **Fix:** cut it, don't rebalance it — rebalancing a mechanic that
  shouldn't exist just delays the cut.
- **Symptom:** the team keeps layering sub-rules and edge cases onto a system that still
  feels bad. **Diagnosis:** this papers over a broken foundational loop instead of fixing
  it, and makes the ruleset more arbitrary with each pass. **Fix:** address the core loop
  directly; stop adding exceptions to it.

## 7. Cases worth citing by name

**Dodo Derby (canceled).** Dodos on flying blocks in an arena above water. The team
believed fun core movement plus a cute skin could substitute for a fantasy — the
"gameplay-first, skin it later" trap. Five mechanical iterations chased the fun without
fixing the actual problem: no recognizable fantasy, a ruleset only the three-person team
could parse. Playtested almost exclusively by that team, who had a good time while
external players stayed confused — a textbook internal echo chamber. Canceled without a
financial trigger, on the standard that a project without a real chance of being
exceptional isn't worth a multi-year commitment even when solvent. Proves: fun mechanics
don't substitute for a fantasy, and a team's own enjoyment while playtesting isn't
evidence the market agrees. A subsequent Steam tag audit of "Gladiator" found a genuine
gap (stylized, responsive, real-time multiplayer arena combat) between a 2D-management
cluster and a physics-ragdoll-sim cluster — market-gap research as a real tool for aiming
a fantasy-first pivot, distinct from picking a fantasy in the abstract.

**Schedule I.** Watering plants, in isolation, is as mundane as mechanics get. Wrapped in
a drug-empire-management fantasy it became a major commercial draw. The proof case for
"you don't need a hook": players shop for the experience a mechanic is dressed as, not the
mechanic.

**Maki's Adventure vs. Paddle Paddle Paddle** (same developer). "Platformer, but you turn
into a shark" underperformed. "You and a friend paddle a boat through lava" sold
considerably better despite simpler visuals. Same team, same execution tier — the pitch
structure was the variable that moved.

**Thronefall.** Months were spent prototyping it as a deckbuilding card game before the
team saw that "ruler of an epic kingdom" clashed with sitting at a table drawing cards.
Dropping the card format for minimalist kingdom-defense made fantasy and mechanics align,
and the design clicked only then — not from any single mechanic improving. The eventual
pitch ("Build and defend your castle," not "drain enemy health bars") is credited as part
of what carried the game past one million copies sold.

**Islanders vs. Oraboros** (same developer). Same marketing effort behind both; Islanders
vastly outsold Oraboros. The gap tracked to visual appeal and readability, not promotional
spend — evidence that Appeal is a property of the concept and its presentation, not
something a marketing budget alone can manufacture.

## 8. Questions to ask a team that cannot choose between concepts

A team stuck between candidates is usually stuck on sunk cost, unequal ownership, or an
unstated reward function, not on merit. Ask, in order:

1. **What are you actually optimizing for** — money, craft, portfolio, or a game that has
   to exist? Two people arguing from different reward functions will never converge by
   debating the concepts; they aren't evaluating the same thing.
2. **Name the primary Magic (section 3) for each candidate, out loud.** A candidate
   nobody can name a Magic for isn't a real candidate yet.
3. **Has each candidate had a cheap, disposable prototype**, built separately by
   different people, or is the preference based on debate alone? Build both cheaply and
   let external reaction pick.
4. **If ownership is split across candidates**, is the disagreement about quality or
   about who has more invested in which prototype? Swapping prototype ownership, or
   splitting domain authority so each lead has unilateral say in their own domain,
   resolves more deadlocks than another round of debate.
5. **Which candidate would you still want to be working on in month twenty?** Use last —
   a tiebreaker only for candidates that have already cleared sections 1 through 6
   equally.

For the underlying argument on whether vision or market data should drive candidate
generation, and how much veto power demand data gets, see `verdicts.md` §1.

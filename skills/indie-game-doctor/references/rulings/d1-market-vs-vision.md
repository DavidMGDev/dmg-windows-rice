# Ruling: Market-first vs vision-first ideation

## The question, sharpened

Both briefs concede more than they contest. The market advocate concedes that Steam data is
backward-looking, survivorship-biased, blind to console and itch, and internally inconsistent across
Zukowski's own talks. The vision advocate concedes that genre demand is real, that it constrains the room,
and that a team must already play the genre it builds in. Both prescribe cheap validation before
commitment. Both treat scope discipline as the answer to runway and burnout. Both agree the 90/10
product-to-promotion figure has no methodology behind it.

What survives is narrower and more useful: **when demand evidence and creative conviction point at
different games, which one gets the veto, and in what order do you consult them.**

Note also that market-first and vision-first both describe operations costing days, not months. The
projects that die do not die from consulting these in the wrong order. They die from spending two years
before showing either one to an outside player. That reframing does most of the reconciliation work below.

## Where each side is right

**Market-first is right about the category.** Baseline demand differs by an order of magnitude between
genres and is knowable before you commit (Zukowski, [Marketing Ladder]). The mechanical thresholds are not
survivorship artifacts: roughly $8,000 gross in the first 24-48 hours triggers New & Trending, and
qualified wishlists convert at 15-25% against 1-5% for unqualified ones. Those describe the funnel every
game passes through, whatever its vision. Tyroller's *Dodo Derby* cancellation ([Our New Game Didn't Meet
Expectations]) is the strongest single case in the corpus: maximum passion, funded, experienced team,
killed by an internal playtest echo chamber, and the recovery direction came from auditing the Gladiator
tag for an unserved middle.

**Vision-first is right about which game inside the category, and about attrition.** Demand data shows the
tag, never the fun engine (IGC, [150 Games] diagnosis #9: survivor-like imitators copying bullet-dodging
while missing that meta-progression drives retention). Copying the visible leader produces GMTK's Lesson 6
failure, the genre-as-checklist derivative. And conviction is an attrition argument rather than a
marketing one: Derek Yu's last 10% consumes 90% of the grind, and wishlists do not carry a team through
month 20.

**Conditions.** Market-first strengthens as runway shortens (under ~18 months), as the reward function
becomes explicitly commercial, on a first or second commercial title, with no existing audience, selling
premium on Steam. Vision-first strengthens with low burn, income from elsewhere, a team of 1-5, a
subtractive scope, and a genre the team plays natively.

## The ruling

**Vision generates the candidates, the market culls them, and an outside player picks the winner. No stage
may be skipped, and the last one outranks the other two.**

Conviction is the only input no competing team has, so it belongs at the generator. Demand data is a
filter with a veto over category, not a brief for the game inside it. Neither one is evidence that anybody
wants your specific game, so both are subordinate to the cheapest real signal available: a stranger's
reaction to a gif, a browser prototype, or a capsule.

A doctor can say it this way: *bring me three ideas you would be glad to spend two years on, and I will
tell you which one the market can afford. Then a stranger tells us which one is actually alive.*

## Why vision goes first in the sequence

Order matters, and the corpus points one way. Market-first ideation searches a space everyone else can
see, which produces the modal Steam outcome: a competent entry in a demand-leading genre, measured against
that genre's apex title, landing in the 0-10 review bracket (Tyroller, [Can You Guess Which Game Is a
Success]). Running the filter after generation costs nothing extra and preserves the one asymmetric input
you have. Running it before generation deletes that input permanently.

This is a sequence preference, not a strength preference. When filter and generator disagree about
category viability, the filter wins.

## Decision rules

1. **If you do not play the target genre for pleasure, do not enter it.** Both sides agree here, which
   makes it the most reliable rule in the debate. Genre illiteracy makes you misread the demand data and
   leaves you unable to tune the nuance (IGC, [Challenge & Difficulty]).
2. **If runway is under 12 months, demand tier gets a hard veto.** Take the vision candidate sitting in a
   viable category. If none of your candidates do, generate more candidates rather than override the veto.
3. **If runway is over 24 months or burn is near zero, vision may override demand tier once**, but only
   with an Appeal test passed first and only with subtractive scope.
4. **If the target genre is on the structurally hard list** (platformer, puzzle, VR, traditional RTS,
   party, shmup, beat-em-up), the entry price is top-1% visual craft. Budget for it explicitly or change
   category. This is Zukowski's rule and neither advocate disputes it.
5. **If your candidate is a demand-leading content-dense genre** (deckbuilders, roguelites, grand strategy,
   city builders, simulation) and you are three people, the demand signal is a trap. You cannot cut the
   expected content volume by 80% without a revolutionary alternative hook, and the same production math
   drives IGC's barren-open-world diagnosis (#8), the most expensive thing a small team can attempt.
6. **If the only evidence for the concept comes from the team playing it, the concept is untested.**
   *Dodo Derby* is what that costs.
7. **If two candidates both clear the demand filter, take the one you would still want in month 20.**
   Excitement is the tiebreaker, never the veto.

## Diagnostic questions

Ask a team stuck between a passion project and a market-shaped project, in this order:

1. What is your reward function here: money, craft, portfolio, or a game that must exist? (Tyroller's
   test. Everything downstream changes with the answer.)
2. How many months of runway, and does this game have to fund the next one?
3. Do you play this genre? When did you last spend 30 hours in it voluntarily?
4. Name the emotion a player should feel in minute one. An answer that names a mechanic or a genre means
   the vision is not yet articulated, and its weakness is being mistaken for market pressure.
5. What is the demand-to-supply ratio in your two nearest tags, and which of your nearest comparables sold
   badly? Bad comparables teach more than good ones.
6. What has an outside player seen, and what did they do? If the answer is nothing, the team is arguing
   about the wrong thing and the whole market/vision question is premature.
7. If you cancelled today, what would you regret losing? When the honest answer is "the sunk months," that
   is a sunk cost wearing a vision's clothes.

## What would change this ruling

- **Evidence that demand tiers move faster than a production cycle.** If genre demand shifts inside two
  years, the filter's veto weakens and vision-first strengthens on every axis.
- **A team with owned reach.** An existing audience or shipped IP supplies the cold-start traffic the
  demand filter proxies for. Vision takes the veto back.
- **A non-Steam primary channel.** The entire demand map is Steam-derived and does not transfer to console,
  mobile, or a storefront with editorial curation.
- **A non-commercial reward function.** If the team is not optimizing revenue, the market filter has no
  standing to veto anything and this debate dissolves.
- **A measured version of the 90/10 split.** Both advocates admit it is rhetorical. At 60/40 the case for
  early and sustained market work strengthens considerably.

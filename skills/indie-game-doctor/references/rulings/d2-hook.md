# Ruling: does an indie game need a hook?

Scope: teams of 1–10, limited runway, Steam-primary revenue.

## 1. The question, sharpened

Both advocates redefine "hook," and they redefine it in opposite directions, so
the apparent disagreement is smaller than the word count suggests.

- The no-hook brief uses **mechanical hook**: Tyroller's "a mechanic that sets
  your game apart and is designed to get you attention," the "[Genre] BUT
  [gimmick]" pitch.
- The hook brief refuses that definition on line 5 and substitutes
  **transmissible difference**: "one difference a stranger can transmit in one
  compressed unit," where the unit may be a sentence, a GIF, a five-second clip,
  or a demo session.

Under those two definitions, the briefs agree on the headline. The hook
advocate concedes it in plain text: "you don't need a novel mechanic. You do
need something a stranger can restate." The no-hook advocate concedes the
mirror image: "You must be *different*. I am arguing the axis is fantasy,
market gap, or execution quality." Both rank mechanical novelty low on their own
lists — Tyroller's archetype #1 is qualified as a bonus layer and never the sole
pitch, and the hook brief actively demotes genre mashup to "a hook *generator*,
not a hook."

**That convergence is the reconciliation.** Mechanical novelty is optional and
has a bad hit rate. A compressible difference of *some* kind is close to
mandatory. Stop arguing about the word.

Three real disagreements survive the definition fix, and they are what this
ruling decides:

1. **Is execution quality alone a viable differentiator?** No-hook says yes:
   best-looking, most polished entry in a high-demand tag. Hook says no, or
   rather that this is already Magic type 1 (distinct aesthetic) wearing a
   disguise, and that a game nobody can restate lands in the Bronze tier
   (0–40 wishlists/week) regardless of polish.
2. **Does Steam's own tag-based discovery substitute for a transmission
   event at cold start?** No-hook: the algorithm indexes category membership
   and conversion, not novelty, so being a legible member of a wanted genre is
   the discovery position. Hook: the algorithm amplifies traffic it already has,
   and rung 1 of the ladder has only two documented doors, viral post or
   streamer demo.
3. **Which axis should a team lead with in ideation?**

## 2. Where each side is right

**The no-hook brief is right about the axis, and its evidence is better.**
Negative evidence outranks case studies here, because both sides admit the
survivorship problem and only one side supplies failures. The ZorArts pair
(*Maki's Adventure*, a textbook "platformer BUT shark," versus *Paddle Paddle
Paddle*, no mechanical novelty and simpler art, which sold much better) is the
closest thing in the corpus to a within-studio A/B. Add *Ovesnova*, *Dodo
Derby*, and the consulting client who bolted complex progression onto a
survivor-like and killed its appeal. That is four documented instances of
mechanic-led differentiation failing, against zero documented instances of
fantasy-led differentiation failing *because it was fantasy-led*. Leading with
the mechanic is the worst-hit-rate route on the evidence available.

Conditions where this is strongest: proven-demand genres where buyers shop by
tag (horror, survivor-likes, city and management sims, RPGs, deckbuilders);
visible loops where a screenshot creates the urge without explanation; first or
second commercial title; a team that needs a game that funds the next game.

**The hook brief is right about the bar, and about cold start.** Its three
strongest pieces of evidence are structural rather than anecdotal, and the
no-hook brief does not answer any of them:

- The **Lucas Pope test** (during prototyping, can you already picture the
  trailer?) is a design verdict derived from a measured constraint, and it
  applies to a fantasy-led game exactly as much as a mechanic-led one.
- The **3–5 second attention budget** and the 0:00–0:15 / 0:15–0:45 trailer
  shape are measurements, not preferences. There is a slot in the artifact and
  something has to go in it.
- The **150-game IGC corpus** is the only thing resembling a sample. Its
  recurring diagnosis for a forgettable submission is not "add content" or
  "polish harder," it is that nothing compresses: geometric character with no
  charm, abstract Sokoban with no theme, diluted clone of a beloved AAA title.

The hook brief also lands a hit the no-hook brief never addresses: **Thronefall
is a contaminated data point.** Tyroller has a large YouTube audience, which
supplies rung 1 without a viral spark. Citing his million-seller as proof that a
cold-start team needs no transmission event is exactly the confound the hook
brief names as its own weakest condition. *Dwarf Fortress* is worse as evidence:
decades of cult word-of-mouth, not a cold Steam launch. Strip those two and the
no-hook brief's positive cases thin out to *Schedule I*, *Islanders*, and
*Stardew Valley*, all three of which compress into one sentence a stranger can
repeat.

Conditions where the hook side is strongest: no existing audience, no publisher,
no prior title; saturated or "struggling" genres (platformer, puzzle, RTS,
shmup) where the stated bar is top-1% visual fidelity; and mastery /
winner-take-all genres where players compare every entry to the apex title.

**Where the no-hook brief overreaches.** "Steam discovery does not index
novelty" is true and beside the point. The algorithm follows revenue and
conversion, which means it amplifies traffic you already have; it does not
manufacture the first thousand impressions. Tag legibility determines *who* sees
you and how well they convert. It does not get you seen. Both mechanisms are
real and they operate at different rungs.

**Where the hook brief overreaches.** Classifying Thronefall's minimalist
low-poly readability as "a hook" makes the claim very hard to falsify, since any
successful game's most distinctive visible property can be relabelled a hook
after the fact. The brief half-admits this. Its offered falsifier (a cold-start
crowded-genre success whose coverage nobody could summarise) is honest but
nearly unobservable, because coverage of a success is written *after* someone
finds the sentence.

## 3. The ruling

**You do not need a novel mechanic, and leading with one is the route with the
worst observed hit rate. You do need one difference a stranger can repeat after
five seconds, and the cheapest, most reliable place to put it is the fantasy and
the art, not the rules.**

Quotable form for the patient: *"Your game doesn't need a gimmick. It needs a
sentence someone else will say out loud. If your capsule and a 5-second clip
don't produce that sentence, that is a design problem, not a marketing problem."*

The preference is stated: on ideation axis, the no-hook brief wins. On whether a
compressible difference is optional, the hook brief wins. Execution quality
alone is ruled **not sufficient at cold start** — polish converts and retains,
it does not spark. Polish is the second half of the job, and a team that spends
its whole budget there ships a game with a healthy wishlist-to-sale rate and no
wishlists.

## 4. Decision rules

1. **If you cannot name which of the six Magics is yours, you do not have one.**
   Distinct aesthetic / high-concept pitch / physics comedy / addictive loop /
   deep systems / starved niche. Pick exactly one as primary. Two is a bonus,
   zero is the Bronze tier. Do not answer "polish" — polish is not on the list.
2. **If your Magic is types 1–3 (screenshot-sellable), spend on capsule, key
   art, and a 30-second trailer.** If it is types 4–6 (demo-sellable), the demo
   *is* your compressed unit: budget for it, get it to genre streamers, and hold
   yourself to the median-playtime floor (25–45 min, sources conflict) rather
   than to wishlist count.
3. **If this is your first or second commercial title, follow the blueprint.**
   Ship a legible entry in a genre with proven organic demand and put your
   differentiation in fantasy, art cohesion, or a market gap. Save mechanical
   invention for title three, when you have an audience to absorb the risk.
4. **If your differentiator is a new mechanic, convert it to a fantasy or drop
   it.** A mechanic that cannot be restated as an experience someone wants
   ("run a drug empire," not "water plants") will not survive a capsule, a
   thumbnail, or a stranger's retelling.
5. **If your genre has an apex title you cannot out-build (Metroidvania vs
   *Hollow Knight*, deckbuilder vs *Balatro*), the execution route is closed.**
   Find a gap or a concept, or change genre. "Better than the leader" is not a
   plan a 1–10 team can fund.
6. **If you already have reach (10k+ engaged followers, a publisher with
   marketing spend, or a prior title with a mailing list), the compression bar
   drops.** You own rung 1. Optimise for conversion and retention instead, and
   ignore rules 1–2's urgency.
7. **If your one-sentence pitch requires a second sentence of setup, it is a
   genre salad.** Cut until one clause survives against a known genre frame.

## 5. Diagnostic tests

Run these before pre-production ends, not after the store page goes up.

- **The retell test.** Show a stranger the capsule and 30 seconds of footage.
  Wait an hour. Ask them to describe the game to a third person. If the
  description is a genre name plus adjectives ("some kind of pixel RPG, looked
  nice"), there is no compressed unit. If it is a sentence with a noun someone
  would repeat, you have one.
- **The 5-second clip test.** Cut a five-second clip with no text. Does a viewer
  ask "what is that?" or say "oh, one of those"? "Oh, one of those" plus a
  desire to click is a pass for types 4–6 delivered by demo. "Oh, one of those"
  plus indifference is [BlowUp]'s killer verdict: "looks okay, but I won't buy it
  right now."
- **The capsule row test.** Put your capsule in a row of twelve real competitors
  from your primary tag, at store thumbnail size. Ask five people which one they
  would click. If you are not picked at above chance, your differentiation is
  invisible where it actually gets spent.
- **The Lucas Pope test.** From the current concept alone, write the trailer's
  0:15–0:45 section. If you cannot fill it without describing systems, the
  concept has no visible difference yet. Fix the concept, not the trailer.
- **The tag test.** Name the three tags a buyer filters by to find you. If no
  combination of existing Steam tags describes your game, you have no
  neighbourhood to be recommended from, and "More Like This" cannot help you.
- **The velocity check (post-launch of the store page).** After the page has had
  real impressions, 0–40 wishlists/week is a Magic diagnosis, not an effort
  diagnosis. Do not respond by posting more. Respond by changing the capsule,
  the art direction, or the concept.
- **The alien check.** Ask a genre fan to explain what is happening in one
  screenshot. If they cannot, novelty is fighting comprehension, and legibility
  wins that fight every time.

## 6. What would change this ruling

- **The ZorArts A/B turning out confounded.** If the two ZorArts titles differed
  materially in budget, launch timing, or art quality, the strongest single
  piece of anti-mechanic evidence weakens, and the ideation-axis preference
  narrows toward a tie.
- **A discovery shift away from capsule click-through.** If Steam's surfaces
  move toward retention-weighted or video-feed recommendation, the compression
  bar drops and the execution-first route gets much cheaper. Watch for it.
- **Genuinely starved demand.** In a beloved abandoned genre with no competent
  current entry, existence is the pitch and the compression requirement collapses
  into "it exists and it's good" (*Stardew Valley*). Verify the starvation with
  actual search and tag-follower data, not vibes; teams routinely mistake "small"
  for "starved."
- **Owned reach at cold start.** Any team arriving with its own audience is
  playing a different game, and the ruling's urgency does not apply to them.
- **Contrary data from your own page.** Healthy wishlist velocity with no
  nameable Magic beats the framework. Believe the numbers.
- **Non-Steam-primary revenue.** Console curation, a subscription deal, or a
  mobile launch changes who does the discovering, and this ruling is scoped to
  Steam.

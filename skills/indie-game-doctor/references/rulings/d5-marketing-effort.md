# Ruling: Product-plus-moments vs sustained marketing systems

## The question, sharpened

The briefs converge on nearly everything that matters and then disagree about a genuine residual.

Agreed by both: the 90/10 product-to-promotion figure has no methodology behind it and should not be
quoted as fact. Follower count is not a deliverable. Qualified wishlists convert at 15-25% against 1-5%
for unqualified ones. Next Fest is a multiplier on existing momentum rather than a demand generator. Most
of the GDC "1 Hour a Day" system is moment-preparation spread across a calendar, with only one of its five
weekly days being social posting, and the product advocate says outright that he endorses it as such.

The residual: **where do the wishlists that the moments multiply actually come from, and does the
marketing work terminate?** The product brief says the store page converts Valve's own traffic and one
good asset starts the chain. The sustained brief says press lists, festival submissions and demo cohorts
are built over months and cannot be conjured in the week you need them.

## The wedge that resolves it: lead time, not effort

The product brief's strongest claim is that the marketing list is short and finite. That is true of the
list and false of the calendar. Every item on it has a lead time that exceeds the moment it serves.

A showcase submission closes months before the showcase. A creator list has to exist before the demo
build goes out 24-48 hours early. Zukowski's own pipeline in the Ladder talk, the source of the 90%
framing, prescribes building wishlist momentum over 6-12 months. His organic traction tiers are quoted as
weekly rates, not totals. A team that treats marketing as four discrete moments discovers at each moment
that its entry ticket needed to be bought earlier.

So both models are wrong in their pure form. Daily posting is not the answer, and neither is a checklist
you execute four times. The right object is **a small standing pipeline whose only job is to make the
moments fire on schedule** — and that pipeline contains almost no social posting.

## The second wedge: what "qualified" actually means

Both briefs claim the 15-25% versus 1-5% conversion split, and both attribute it to channel. The product
brief says demos produce qualified wishlists and social produces unqualified ones. The sustained brief
says Discord and niche communities produce qualified ones.

Neither reading is right. Qualification comes from **whether the person has seen the game move**, not from
which platform they arrived on. A demo player who came from Twitter is qualified. A giveaway entrant from
your Discord is not. Someone who watched forty seconds of gameplay footage and wishlisted is closer to
qualified than someone who liked a screenshot.

That reframes the whole allocation question into one rule: every marketing hour should end in a stranger
watching gameplay or playing the game. Hours that end in an impression are close to worthless, whichever
brief prescribed them.

## The ruling

**Marketing is a small permanent pipeline, not a habit and not an event. Budget roughly 10-15% of one
person's week from the day the Steam page goes up, spend almost none of it on social posting, and spend
all of it on things with lead times: capture, creator and press lists, festival submissions, and a demo
you keep testing.**

Stage allocation for a typical small team:

| Stage | Marketing share of one person's week | What it buys |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prototype / pre-production | ~0 hours | Only the Lucas Pope test: if you cannot already picture the trailer, change the concept. Free, and it is a design decision. |
| Production, 12+ months out | 1-2 hours | Steam page live early (highest-return single act, it starts Valve's own traffic converting), routine capture, creator/press list started. |
| 6-12 months out | 4-5 hours | Demo built and iterated to median playtime, festival and showcase submissions, playtest recruitment pipeline. |
| Next Fest month, launch month | Most of the team, 2-4 weeks | The moments themselves. |

The product brief is right that marginal hours beyond this belong in the game. The sustained brief is
right that these particular hours cannot be deferred to the moment they serve.

For a doctor's use: *five hours a week, none of it posting. If an hour does not end with a stranger
watching your game move, it was not marketing.*

## Where each side is right

**Product-plus-moments is right** for Category A games whose magic is one shareable visual or physics
moment, where a single asset can produce thousands of wishlists in 48 hours; for teams in high-demand
genres where Steam's own traffic is the largest available channel; for runways under 24 months, where an
audience you do not have will not mature in time; and about the Islanders versus Oraboros comparison,
which is the closest thing in the corpus to a controlled test and points hard at product.

**Sustained is right** for first commercial titles with no list of any kind, for Category B
"playing is believing" games where screenshots structurally cannot sell the appeal, for struggling genres
that will not be handed a showcase slot, for games with an identifiable subculture or adjacent non-gaming
audience, and for anyone crowdfunding. Its strongest evidence is the Paranormal Tales chain, where each
rung was only reachable because the previous one had been worked.

## Decision rules

1. **Put the Steam page up as early as you have one good screenshot and a paragraph.** It converts traffic
   Valve already sends and starts the wishlist clock. Nothing else on this list outranks it.
2. **If your game's appeal is invisible in a screenshot, you have a design problem, not a marketing
   problem.** Tactics games, deckbuilders and visual novels hide their fun inside the player's head. The
   documented fix is a design change that externalizes it, like Firewatch's timer bar.
3. **If you have under 2,000 wishlists a month before Next Fest, do not enter.** It multiplies momentum
   and a multiplier on a small number stays small. Delay and build the operand.
4. **Build the creator and press list continuously, a few contacts a week**, and reach out individually
   about a month before a demo launch or Next Fest. Send builds 24-48 hours early. The corpus gives no
   figure for how far ahead the list itself must start; it treats list-building as a standing weekly task,
   which is the reason it cannot be produced on demand.
5. **If a marketing activity does not end in someone watching gameplay, cut it.** That test disposes of
   most of what teams call marketing.
6. **If the core loop is broken, stop marketing entirely and fix it.** Both briefs concede this. Marketing
   a game with a 10-minute median playtime converts strangers into people who know your game is thin.

## What not to spend marketing time on

- **Daily social posting for its own sake.** It produces impressions, the one input Steam already supplies
  for free at 35-40M concurrent users.
- **Follower-count chasing.** Followers convert at the unqualified 1-5% rate, against 15-25% for someone
  who has seen the game move. The hour spent putting people into a demo outperforms the hour spent
  gaining followers by that ratio.
- **Audience polling on design decisions.** Letting followers vote on mechanics, UI or art produces
  average, appeal-to-nobody results, which is the apathy that actually kills indie games.
- **Giveaways and key-drops for wishlist count.** They inflate the number that looks good and deflate the
  conversion rate that decides your first 48 hours.
- **Cold press kits to outlets that do not cover your genre.** The corpus rates press outreach as an
  optional side quest relative to creator outreach.
- **Conventions, pre-launch, on a small budget.** High cost, low measured yield in every source here.

## What would change this ruling

- **A team with owned reach**, a sequel, or publisher PR. They already have the operand and can drop to
  the moments-only model without penalty.
- **A measured 90/10 split.** Every argument on the product side leans on a figure both advocates admit is
  rhetorical. At 60/40 the pipeline should be considerably larger.
- **Attribution data from the GDC cadence.** That talk reports a system and no outcomes: no wishlist
  figures, no revenue, no attribution. Published results would move this ruling in whichever direction
  they pointed.
- **A change in how Steam weights discovery.** The entire moments model is downstream of a
  revenue-velocity-driven algorithm. If discovery became retention-weighted or curation-led, launch-week
  concentration would stop being the right shape.

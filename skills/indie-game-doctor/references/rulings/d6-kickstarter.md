# Ruling: Crowdfunding as a small-team funding route

## The question, sharpened

Both briefs accept the same arithmetic, which is unusual and makes this the most decidable debate in the
set.

Agreed: Kickstarter's cut is ~8% against Steam's 30%. A $10k-$50k campaign does not fund multi-year
production. Campaign plus development runs ~5x the anticipated work and needs 2-4 months of prep before a
~28-day live period. Key redemptions generate $0 Steam revenue and land reviews in the unweighted "Other"
bucket. Setting the goal below true cost is a documented route to mid-production collapse. And both sides
agree the position flips at megahit scale ($500k+, 20k-40k backers), where volume brute-forces past the
algorithmic penalty.

The pro brief's own §8 concedes the anti brief's exact configuration. The anti brief's "weakest when"
section concedes the pro brief's exact configuration. **They have already agreed on the answer and are
arguing about which configuration is typical.**

So the ruling is not "yes" or "no." It is a set of gates, plus one number that decides the case.

## The number that decides it

The anti brief's strongest move is the size collision: ~500 backers is the canonical small campaign, and
~530 units at $15 is what clears the ~$8,000 New & Trending bar in 24-48 hours. Those are the same people.
The cohort you converted is the qualified cohort, converting at 15-25% against 1-5%.

The pro brief's answer is that the campaign is also an awareness beat producing wishlists from non-backers.
That is true and it is not a defence unless you count it. Both briefs treat it as unmeasurable. It is not.

**Wishlists per backer is the decisive ratio, and it is observable while the campaign runs.**

Work the arithmetic forward. Backers already own the game, so launch-day units must come entirely from
non-backer wishlists. At a 15-25% first-week conversion on qualified wishlists, clearing 530 units needs
roughly **2,100-3,500 non-backer wishlists**. A campaign that ends with 500 backers and 3,000 net-new
wishlists has paid for the cohort it spent. A campaign that ends with 500 backers and 700 wishlists has
sold your launch for $9,200 gross.

Six wishlists per backer is the break-even shape. Below that, the campaign is a pre-sale wearing a
marketing campaign's clothes.

## The second axis: distance to launch

Cannibalization is a function of proximity, and neither brief says so.

Zukowski's finding that wishlist decay is a myth cuts both ways. It means a wishlist collected during a
campaign survives intact to launch day, which favours running the campaign early. It also means a backer
converted today is permanently subtracted from launch day, however far away that is.

The asymmetry is in what happens between. A campaign 24+ months out sells to an audience you will spend two
years re-recruiting from press, festivals, Next Fest and a demo. A campaign 6 months out sells the exact
cohort you were about to convert, with no time left to rebuild it. Same mechanism, very different bill.

## The ruling

**Do not crowdfund to fund the game. Crowdfund only when the alternative actually on the table is worse
than losing your launch-day cohort — and if you run one, run it early, size the goal at true cost, and
count non-backer wishlists as the campaign's real deliverable.**

The money is not the case for it. Both briefs agree $10k-$50k does not fund multi-year production, and it
sits against a ~$150,000 six-month viability threshold. What the campaign genuinely buys is a
non-dilutive alternative to a bad contract, and the corpus contains one case that carries this on its own:
*The Guest*, a 100%-recoup deal signed at zero cash reserves, 25,000+ copies over three years, $0 to the
developer. The GDC panel's own stated lesson prescribes micro-scoped crowdfunding as the thing that
prevents that signature.

That is the honest shape of the pro case. It is a defence against a worse instrument, not a growth
strategy — and the anti brief concedes it in those terms.

For a doctor's use: *a campaign is not income, it is a pre-sale of your best 500 customers. Charge enough
for them, and check the grant window first.*

## Check the cheaper doors first, in this order

1. **Regional and national grants, prototype grants, incubators.** Non-dilutive, no key liability, no
   fulfillment obligation, no five-month campaign. This is the anti brief's cleanest hit and the pro brief
   does not answer it.
2. **Bootstrapping from contract or side-project work.** Slow, and it costs no launch cohort.
3. **Three browser-playable Itch prototypes over three months.** If the argument is validation rather than
   money, this is the cheaper instrument by an order of magnitude. Browser builds draw ~10x the engagement
   of downloads, and the rule is that people who will not play it free will not pay $15-20.
4. **A demo holding a 25-minute median playtime.** Proves the loop works without pre-selling anybody.
5. *Then* a campaign.

## Gates — all must clear

1. **An existing audience that can carry days 1-3.** The U-curve is structural: the opening surge comes
   from followers and friends, and the mid-campaign plateau is survivable only if the opening was strong.
   Never launch cold.
2. **A goal set at true production cost**, including a marketing line and post-funding fulfillment. A goal
   you are afraid to name means the scope is wrong, not the funding method. Publish the budget breakdown.
3. **A mechanic that GIFs well, plus a playable demo.** Long-form strategy and slow narrative campaign
   badly and should weight accordingly.
4. **Digital and credit tiers only**, unless the full physical pipeline — MOQs, packaging labour, shipping,
   customs, fulfillment delay — is costed in advance. The margin lives in the $100-$200+ tiers, and the
   common documented error is underpricing them out of discomfort.
5. **One clear prepared month**, with updates pre-written, contacts listed, media packages pre-composed and
   community shifts rotated. The 5x multiplier applies to spontaneous campaigns; every named mitigation
   moves work out of the live 28 days.
6. **Enough runway to reach launch without the money.** If the campaign failing kills the project, you are
   not funding development, you are gambling it.

## Structural mitigations

These follow from the mechanism rather than from any source, so treat them as reasoning rather than
reported practice.

- **Run it early.** 18+ months before launch, so there is time to rebuild the cohort you spent.
- **Track net-new wishlists daily during the campaign**, not just pledges. The ratio is your only live
  read on whether the trade is working, and it is knowable by day 5.
- **Recount your launch threshold after subtracting backers.** Whatever wishlist target you had, add
  ~6x your backer count to it. If that number is out of reach, the campaign is the wrong instrument.
- **Do not stack the campaign against Next Fest.** Both are momentum events drawing on the same list, and
  the second one will read as flat.

## Where each side is right

**Anti is right** for the modal case: 1-10 people, Steam-primary, premium PC at $10-20, a $10k-$50k goal,
and a small engaged audience that would plausibly buy on day one. The mechanism is causal rather than
correlational — Valve's algorithm follows gross revenue, a key hands it nothing, and ~16 hours of weak
velocity drops the title out of New & Trending. It is also right about the evidence: the six-funded-devs
sample is selected on the outcome being argued for, and not one of the six reports Steam launch revenue.

**Pro is right** at scale ($500k+), off Steam (console, direct, physical), when the alternative is a
100%-recoup deal or not shipping at all, and about the artifact reuse — the demo, the Discord, the press
list and the mailing list all outlive the campaign and are the same assets the marketing pipeline needs
anyway. It is also right that a failed campaign costs 2-4 months while a failed vertical slice costs $45k
and six months.

## What would change this ruling

- **Measured wishlist-per-backer data across campaigns.** The entire ruling turns on a ratio nobody in this
  corpus has published. Real numbers would settle it in one direction or the other.
- **A change in how Valve weights key activations.** If redemptions carried transaction signal or "Other"
  reviews carried algorithmic weight, the cannibalization mechanism disappears and the anti case collapses
  to a workload argument.
- **A team whose primary channel is not Steam.** The whole debate is downstream of one storefront's
  discovery algorithm.
- **Grant availability.** The anti brief's first rebuttal assumes a grant window exists. In regions with
  no arts or games funding, crowdfunding may genuinely be the only non-dilutive door, and gate 1 of the
  cheaper-doors list is unavailable rather than merely unattractive.

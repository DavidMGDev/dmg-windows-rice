# Verdicts

Nine questions in this field get contradictory answers from credible practitioners. Each
was argued out by opposed advocates and then reconciled into one working position, with a
stated preference wherever the sources genuinely conflict. None of them is an average of
the two sides.

Use these whenever two sources disagree, whenever a team is deadlocked, or whenever
someone quotes a rule at you that the situation does not fit. Full arguments, conditions,
and the evidence that would overturn each one are in `rulings/`.

---

## 1. Market-first or vision-first ideation?

**Vision generates the candidates, the market culls them, and an outside player picks the
winner. No stage may be skipped, and the last one outranks the other two.**

Conviction is the only input no competing team has, so it belongs at the generator.
Demand data has a veto over category and no standing on the game inside it.
Neither is evidence that anyone wants your specific game, so both are subordinate to a
stranger's reaction to a gif, a browser build, or a capsule.

Running the filter after generation costs nothing and preserves your one asymmetric
input. Running it before generation deletes that input permanently, which produces the
modal Steam outcome: a competent entry in a demand-leading genre, measured against that
genre's apex title.

Say it as: *bring me three ideas you would be glad to spend two years on, and I will tell
you which one the market can afford. Then a stranger tells us which one is alive.*

- If you do not play the target genre for pleasure, do not enter it.
- Runway under 12 months: demand tier gets a hard veto.
- Runway 12-24 months, the modal case: no override in either direction. Run the three
  stages in order and let the stranger break the tie.
- Runway over 24 months or near-zero burn: vision may override demand tier once, with an
  appeal test passed first and subtractive scope.
- Structurally hard genres (platformer, puzzle, VR, traditional RTS, party, shmup,
  beat-em-up) charge top-1% visual craft as the entry price. Budget it or change category.
- Demand-leading content-dense genres (deckbuilders, roguelites, grand strategy, city
  builders, simulation) are a trap for three people. Content generosity is the competitive
  axis in all of them, so the demand signal is real and unreachable at your headcount.
- Two candidates both clear the filter: take the one you would still want in month 20.
  Excitement is the tiebreaker, never the veto.

## 2. Does a game need a hook?

**You do not need a novel mechanic, and leading with one has the worst observed hit rate.
You do need one difference a stranger can repeat after five seconds, and the cheapest
place to put it is the fantasy and the art, not the rules.**

Execution quality alone is ruled insufficient at cold start. Polish converts and retains;
it does not spark. A team that spends its whole budget there ships a game with a healthy
wishlist-to-sale rate and no wishlists.

Say it as: *your game doesn't need a gimmick. It needs a sentence someone else will say
out loud. If your capsule and a five-second clip don't produce that sentence, that is a
design problem, not a marketing problem.*

- Name which of the six Magics is yours: distinct aesthetic, high-concept pitch, physics
  comedy, addictive loop, deep systems, starved niche. One primary, two is a bonus, zero
  is the bottom tier. "Polish" is not on the list.
- Magic types 1-3 are screenshot-sellable: spend on capsule, key art, a 30-second trailer.
  Types 4-6 are demo-sellable: the demo is your compressed unit, and median playtime is
  your metric rather than wishlist count.
- First or second commercial title: follow the blueprint. Put differentiation in fantasy,
  art cohesion, or a market gap. Save mechanical invention for title three.
- A differentiator that is a mechanic must convert to a fantasy or be dropped. "Run a drug
  empire," not "water plants."
- If the genre has an apex title you cannot out-build, the execution route is closed.
- If the one-sentence pitch needs a second sentence of setup, it is a genre salad.

## 3. Design theory and planning, or prototyping and iteration?

**Build to find out what is true. Use theory to find out what it means. Write down only
what your future self or a second person will need to act on.**

Authority splits by question. "Is this fun, does this feel right, will people play it"
belongs to the build, always, and no framework overrules a playtest on those. "Why did
this fail and what should we try next" belongs to theory, because a build answers it only
by accident. Sequence theory *between* iterations rather than before them: on iteration
one you have nothing to diagnose, so build; from iteration two, name the cause first.

Say it as: *your prototype is the experiment and your framework is the hypothesis. A team
with no hypothesis is guessing repeatedly and calling the repetition rigor.*

- Two people disagree on a design decision: build both cheaply instead of arguing. Celeste
  settled double jump versus air dash in thirty minutes each.
- A playtest fails: name the cause category before touching the build. Barrier to play,
  challenge quality, difficulty quantity, or cognitive load. Four incompatible fixes.
- Every small change makes it worse: you are in a local minimum. The exit is a big cheap
  jump reusing existing assets. Theory tells you to jump, only a build tells you where you
  landed.
- **Write a kill criterion before any prototype longer than two weeks.** One sentence, one
  date, decided while still unattached to the answer. Highest-return writing in indie dev.
- Scale documentation to readers, not ambition. Solo: vision sentence, kill criteria, task
  list. Two to three: add pillars stated as emotions and an MVP boundary. Four or more, or
  remote, or external money: a minimalist living GDD with modal verbs (WILL locked, SHOULD
  open to playtest, COULD exploratory).
- Never a lore bible before the loop is validated. Never pillars before the first
  prototypes. Pillars describe something you have already found, so they filter candidates
  rather than generate them.

**Failure modes are symmetric and a team usually has exactly one.** Under-theorized:
reactive changes, quoting the last playtester, the same problem returning under new names.
Over-planned: the document is longer than the build, mechanics defended by reference to a
plan rather than a session. Prescribe the opposite of whichever you see.

## 4. Radical scope constraint or ambition-led scope?

**Minimize production surface as hard as the vision survives. Let player-facing depth be
set by what the genre expects and the price demands. Buy the gap with combinatorics, not
with headcount.**

"Small" was doing two jobs. *Production surface* is how many things you build and
maintain. *Player-facing depth* is hours, replayability, mastery ceiling. Every game cited
as proof that small games sell is small in surface and large in depth: Thronefall has no
character rigs and hundreds of hours of community playtime.

| | Shallow depth | Deep depth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Small surface** | No viable price. The itch-shaped game. | **The target.** Thronefall, Balatro, Mini Metro. |
| **Large surface** | Barren open world. The homework prototype. | Needs a studio you do not have. |

Say it as: *your budget is the number of things you have to build. Your target is the
number of hours the genre expects. If those two numbers do not meet, the answer is more
interaction between fewer parts, not more parts.*

- Audit production surface before arguing about size. Any discipline with no owner is the
  first cut, not a hiring problem.
- Set the depth target from the genre's floor, not from preference. If you cannot meet it,
  change genre. Losing a content arms race is worse than not entering.
- 20 isolated elements yield ~20 strategies at full production cost each. 10 interacting
  elements yield 100+ at roughly half the asset cost. Depth bought as content is the
  expensive version of something combinatorics sells cheaply.
- Every cut needs an artistic reason. Unjustified limits read as cheap; themed ones read as
  style. Mini Metro's diagram aesthetic deleted the character pipeline and sold the fantasy
  better than characters would have.
- Grow in six-week increments gated on steep returns, never on calendar or ambition.
- Estimation error is proportional: a six-month scope takes a year, a one-month scope takes
  two months. Only shrinking the baseline shrinks the absolute overrun.
- Reaching for crowdfunding or a publisher to close a scope gap means the scope is wrong.
- Zero burn rate changes the answer. Five people on payroll cannot call it ambition.

## 5. Product-plus-moments or sustained marketing?

**Marketing is a small permanent pipeline. Budget roughly 10-15% of one person's week from
the day the Steam page goes up, spend almost none of it on social posting, and spend all of
it on things with lead times: capture, creator and press lists, festival submissions, and a
demo you keep testing.**

The marketing to-do list is short and finite. The *calendar* is not, because every item on
it has a lead time longer than the moment it serves. A team treating marketing as four
discrete events discovers at each event that its entry ticket needed buying earlier.

The qualified-wishlist split (15-25% against 1-5%) is not about channel. **Qualification
means the person has seen the game move.** A demo player who came from Twitter is
qualified; a giveaway entrant from your Discord is not.

| Stage | Marketing share of one week | What it buys |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prototype | ~0 hours | Only the Lucas Pope test: if you cannot picture the trailer, change the concept. |
| Production, 12+ months out | 1-2 hours | Steam page live early, routine capture, creator list started. |
| 6-12 months out | 4-5 hours | Demo iterated to median playtime, festival submissions, playtest pipeline. |
| Next Fest and launch month | Most of the team, 2-4 weeks | The moments themselves. |

Say it as: *five hours a week, none of it posting. If an hour does not end with a stranger
watching your game move, it was not marketing.*

- Steam page up as early as you have one screenshot and a paragraph. Nothing outranks it.
- Appeal invisible in a screenshot is a design problem. Tactics games, deckbuilders and
  visual novels hide fun inside the player's head; the fix externalizes it, as Firewatch's
  timer bar did.
- Under 2,000 wishlists a month before Next Fest: do not enter. Build the operand.
- Build the creator and press list weekly from the day the page is live, and reach out
  individually about a month before a demo launch or Next Fest. Send builds 24-48 hours
  ahead. A list assembled in a sprint is a list of strangers.
- Broken core loop: stop marketing entirely. See gate 1 in SKILL.md.

## 6. Should a small team crowdfund?

**Do not crowdfund to fund the game. Crowdfund only when the alternative actually on the
table is worse than losing your launch-day cohort. If you run one, run it early, size the
goal at true cost, and count non-backer wishlists as the real deliverable.**

The money is not the case for it: $10k-$50k does not fund multi-year production against a
~$150,000 six-month viability threshold. What a campaign genuinely buys is a non-dilutive
alternative to a bad contract. *The Guest* signed a 100%-recoup deal at zero cash reserves
and took $0 from 25,000+ copies over three years.

**The deciding number is wishlists per backer.** Backers already own the game, so launch
units come entirely from non-backers. At 15-25% conversion, clearing ~530 units needs
roughly 2,100-3,500 non-backer wishlists. Six per backer is break-even. Below that, the
campaign is a pre-sale of your best-converting cohort wearing a marketing campaign's
clothes.

Cannibalization also scales with proximity. A campaign 18+ months out sells an audience you
will spend that time re-recruiting. A campaign six months out sells the exact cohort you
were about to convert, with no time to rebuild it.

Say it as: *a campaign is a pre-sale of your best 500 customers. Charge enough for them,
and check the grant window first.*

Cheaper doors, in order: regional and national grants, prototype grants, incubators →
bootstrapping from contract work → three browser prototypes over three months → a demo
holding a 25-minute median → then a campaign.

Gates, all of which must clear: an existing audience that can carry days 1-3; a goal at
true cost with a published breakdown; a mechanic that gifs well plus a playable demo;
digital and credit tiers only unless the full physical pipeline is costed; one clear
prepared month with updates pre-written; and enough runway to reach launch without the
money.

## 7. Difficulty, accessibility, and player power

Three tensions, three defaults.

**Floor height.** Low floor, high ceiling, implemented as tiered success on shared content,
not as separate difficulty content and not as a second set of levels. Write down one
number: **minutes from first launch to the player's first self-attributed success.** If
playtime comes from content volume, that number must be under ~10 minutes. If it comes
from mastery repetition of a compact space, 30-60 minutes is allowed, but only if every
minute produces attributable failure and the store page says so.

**Assist modes.** Yes, on the Celeste model, restricted to scalar knobs on existing
systems: speed multiplier, damage multiplier, widened timing window, infinite stamina,
retry count. If a toggle needs bespoke level logic or a second enemy behaviour path, cut
it. That is content, not accessibility, and you will not test it. Default off, intent
stated on the screen where they are enabled, excluded from leaderboards. Input remapping,
colour-independent readability, subtitle scaling and hold-vs-toggle are not assist modes
and are not up for debate; they are barriers.

**The broken tennis racket.** For every "too hard" report, ask the tester to narrate their
last three failures out loud.

- They name a cause ("I got greedy", "mistimed the dodge") → intended challenge. You may
  now consider tuning quantity.
- They say "I don't know", "it just hit me", "the controls", or describe something the game
  did not do → barrier. Fix responsiveness, telegraphing, contrast, camera. Re-test. **Do
  not touch a number.**

Barriers are done when about four of five blind testers attribute most of their deaths.
That is the gate onto the difficulty spreadsheet, and before it you should not be building
balance data pipelines at all. This is the single highest-yield diagnostic in the set and
it costs one playtest question.

## 8. Playtesting methodology

Five tensions resolved into one protocol. Standing rules, no exceptions:

1. **The build is never frozen between testers.** Trivial defect: fix now. Design defect
   with a clear diagnosis: fix now and re-test on the next tester. Taste, difficulty, or
   pillars: wait for wave consensus.
2. **Explain nothing.** Two permitted lines: "it's not finished yet" and session logistics.
   Watermark the build "Pre-Alpha — Everything Subject to Change."
3. **Record video plus live audio.** No star ratings, no questionnaires as the primary
   instrument.
4. **Watch hands and eyes, not lips.** Grip, sighs, eye wandering, skipped dialogue,
   running past objectives, repeated failed inputs, hesitation at splits, the quit moment.
5. **The muted-video test gates every change.** Behaviour: act. Words proposing a fix:
   extract the friction, discard the proposal.
6. **Act on consensus of three or more, not on incidents.** Defects need no consensus.
7. **Test in the setting that matches intended play.** Expo footage is marketing data.
8. **Every session runs the whole build.** No UI-only or combat-only sessions.
9. **Sessions are the schedule.** A booked playtest is an immovable milestone against scope
   creep. End every two-week sprint in a testable build.

One line: *watch, don't talk. Fix defects instantly, taste never. Trust what players do,
discard what they propose — except when they invent a goal you never designed, and then
follow it.*

Full protocol by stage: `rulings/r2-playtesting.md`, and `playtesting.md` for the rest.

## 9. The numbers

Every Steam benchmark in this corpus comes from outside observation, not platform data.
Several conflict with each other, and three are resolved rather than averaged:

- **Demo median playtime, 25 versus 45 minutes.** Not a conflict. 25 minutes is the
  completion test on a 20-25 minute demo. 45 minutes is the replay test, and applies only
  where replayability is the product. Under 15 minutes is an alarm on any demo.
- **Wishlist target, 7,000 versus 30,000 versus 100,000.** Three different questions.
  Derive your own floor from price: units needed for ~$8,000 gross, divided by an assumed
  10% launch-window conversion, times three for margin.
- **Discovery Queue share, 20% versus 50%.** Decision-irrelevant. Do not cite either.

And the **90/10 product-to-promotion split has no methodology behind it.** Every advocate
on every side conceded this. Never quote it as fact.

Full audit with confidence tiers, plus the list of numbers that do not exist: `numbers.md`.
